### Welcome to Part 1 : ) 


All the Souls games build their worlds out of many separate maps rather than one giant continuous space. What feels like a seamless journey is actually a careful illusion.

Each map has an internal name that looks something like `m_AA_BB_CC_DD`. You never see this as a player, but it quietly decides how the world is organized.

The first part, **AA**, groups maps into what the game considers an *area*. Every map inside the same area shares things like textures and lighting. This is why places feel visually consistent even when you move through different sections.

The **BB** part splits that area into smaller chunks. You can think of these as blocks. Each block is its own self-contained piece of the world, with its own geometry, collisions, and paths for enemies. Even though two blocks might look connected in-game, behind the scenes they are separate spaces loaded and unloaded as needed.

The last number is usually used for small variations. In **Dark Souls**, this is how the game swaps in DLC versions of areas. In **Bloodborne**, it is sometimes used for time-of-day changes. Same place, slightly different rules.

When a map loads, the game does a lot more than just draw walls and floors.

First, there is the *layout*. This decides where the ground is, where objects sit, where enemies stand, and where invisible regions trigger events. This is why stepping into certain spots suddenly spawns enemies, plays music, or starts a boss fight.

Then come the *textures*. Entire areas share massive texture collections so the game does not have to reload everything constantly. This is part of why transitions inside an area feel smooth, while moving between areas often involves elevators, fog gates, or long corridors.

Next are the *models*. Every building, bridge, and piece of scenery is its own model, even if you experience it as one space. The game assembles these like a set, choosing what to load based on where you are.

Collision is handled separately. This is the invisible version of the world that decides where you can walk, where you slide, and where you fall. It is powered by the **Havok** system, and it is far stricter than the visuals. That is why some surfaces look safe but are not, and others feel solid even when they appear uneven.

Enemies rely on another hidden layer entirely: navigation data. This tells them where they can walk, climb, chase, or give up. Without it, enemies simply would not function. It is also believed this layer helps the game decide *when* to load nearby parts of the world, which is why enemies often appear only when you cross certain invisible lines.

So a TLDR if you got bombed - Souls games break the world into small maps grouped by areas that share textures and lighting. Each map block has its own models, collision, and enemy navigation, and is loaded or unloaded as you move. Physics and collision are handled by **Havok**, while separate navigation data controls enemy movement and awareness.

In Souls games, every part of a map is controlled by a simple question: *should this exist right now?*

Map pieces, collision, objects, enemies, and even the player are all assigned to small sets of invisible groups. These groups decide whether something is **enabled** based on where the player is standing.

When you stand on certain ground, the game checks its **draw groups**. Anything that matches those groups gets turned on. If it does not match, it simply does not exist at that moment.

For collision, there is an extra layer. The ground you are standing on also passes along **display groups**, which decide what *you* are allowed to see and interact with while standing there. This is how the game quietly controls what loads around you as you move.

“Enabled” here means more than just visible. If something is enabled, it is rendered *and* updated. Animations run. Enemies think. Scripts trigger. If it is not enabled, it is completely frozen and invisible.

This is why placement matters so much. If a map piece or enemy is assigned the wrong groups, it might never appear, even though it technically exists in the map. The game is not broken, it is just refusing to turn it on.

This system is also how the games stay efficient. Only what matters near the player is active at any given moment.

One important exception is **Elden Ring**. Its open world does not use this group system. Instead, it loads the world in large chunks with different levels of detail depending on where you are. However, legacy dungeons inside Elden Ring still use the same draw and display group rules.

From **Demon’s Souls** onward, every Souls game relies on this system. You never see it as a player, but it quietly decides what parts of the world are alive around you at all times.

Here’s a **simplified, player-first explanation** that keeps the logic but doesn’t feel like engine documentation.

---

When you click a map piece or collision in **DSMapStudio**, you will notice a grid of small checkboxes, some green, red, or yellow. This is the game’s way of deciding what should exist around the player at any given moment.

Every object in a map (floors, walls, enemies, props, even the player) belongs to invisible groups. Think of these groups as on/off switches. Each object has two sets of switches:

* **Draw groups**, which decide if something *can* be shown
* **Display groups**, which decide what the *player is allowed to see* based on where they are standing

In DSMapStudio, the colors help you read this at a glance:

* A **checkmark** means you are choosing to render things that use this group
* **Green** means the selected object has this draw group enabled
* **Red** means it has this display group enabled
* **Yellow** means it has both enabled, which is usually what you want

Under the hood, this is all just numbers, but the visual view makes it much easier to understand which parts of the world are meant to be active together.

So how does this actually work in-game?

As you move, the game checks the collision you are standing on. The player and enemies inherit the draw groups from that ground. The player also inherits its display groups. The game then compares those groups to nearby objects. If an object shares *any* matching group with what the player can currently see, it is enabled. If not, it is completely disabled.

Enabled means more than visible. Animations run. Enemies think. Scripts trigger. Disabled objects are frozen and invisible, even though they technically still exist.

This system is simple, but it has sharp edges.

If a piece of ground does not allow itself to be drawn, standing on it can cause the player to disappear entirely, effectively softlocking the game. This is why collisions usually need at least one shared draw and display group. In DSMapStudio, yellow boxes are your safety net.

Another common issue happens between adjacent pieces of ground. If two neighboring collisions do not allow each other to be drawn, the player can blink out of existence for a frame while transitioning between them. The fix is simple: make sure nearby collisions are allowed to see each other.

One important detail: collision always works, even if it is not being rendered. This prevents situations where enemies or the player suddenly fall through the world just because something was temporarily disabled.

There are also a couple of special cases worth knowing. Objects and enemies can be told to copy their render groups from another part when they spawn. Objects keep those groups forever, while enemies eventually update their groups based on the ground they stand on. Interestingly, parts with no draw groups set often end up always appearing, acting like global pieces of the world.

You never notice any of this while playing. You just feel that the world is stable, consistent, and intentional. But behind the scenes, it is this quiet group system deciding what parts of the world are alive around you at every step.

To place sound or visual effects directly into the world, we use the map editor in **DSMapStudio**.

First, open the Map Editor and make sure *Regions* are visible. In the menu bar, go to **Display → Object Types → Region** and ensure it is checked. Without this, SFX regions will exist but be completely invisible.

<img width="243" height="229" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/0ff034cb-35d1-4cd4-be5f-0b8c7603ffab" />


Next, load the map you want to work with and move the camera to the exact spot where you want the effect to appear. This could be a throne, a doorway, a bonfire area, or any location where an ambient effect would make sense.



To create the effect, go to **Create → Regions → SFX**. In Dark Souls 1, this option lives under **Events** instead of Regions. When you create it, the SFX region will spawn at the world origin at coordinates (0, 0, 0), far away from where you are currently looking.


<img width="1568" height="933" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/8bc3d85f-8e3a-4905-8824-0dd68633a20e" />

Press **X** on your keyboard to snap the new SFX region into your camera view. From here, you can position it exactly where you want the effect to play.

Once placed, save your changes and reload the area. You can do this by resting at a bonfire or grace, fast traveling, quitting and reloading, or restarting the game. This reload step is required every time you create a new SFX region or change its effect ID, since the game only reads these changes when the area loads.


### Map Contents: What a Map Is Made Of

Once a map is loaded in **DSMapStudio**, you will see the *Map Contents* window fill up. This is a tree-style list that shows everything the game considers part of that map. Every entry in this list is a real object the game knows about.

<img width="672" height="827" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/b0b10a5b-6137-4782-81e1-be2be13cd7ed" />

There are four core types of map objects.

**Events** are the game’s logic glue. They link things together, like telling the game that an object should behave as a chest, a door, or a trigger.

**Lights** are exactly what they sound like. These control specific light sources such as torches, spotlights, or directional lighting. Ambient lighting is handled elsewhere, so these are for intentional highlights.

**Parts** make up the physical world. This includes enemies, terrain, buildings, props, and the invisible collision that decides where you can walk.

**Regions** define spaces rather than objects. They are used for detection, spawning enemies, triggering events, or marking important points in the world.

Each game has its own variations and sub-types under these categories, but at a high level, almost everything you interact with fits into one of these four groups.



### Selecting Map Objects

Before you can inspect or change anything, you need to select it.

You can do this in two ways:

* Click its entry in the Map Contents list
* Click the object directly in the 3D viewport

You are not limited to one object at a time. Holding **Ctrl** lets you select multiple objects individually. Holding **Shift** lets you select a whole range at once from the list.

Once selected, you are no longer looking at the world as a player. You are looking at it as a collection of intentional pieces, each with a role in how the game behaves.
